# Cognitive-science / sports-psychology foreperiod (FP) literature: verified notes

Tags (repo rule, literature-verification skill): [V-FT] checked in full text (quote, table or section given); [V-FT*] full text read through a fetch tool's summary with quoted sentences (re-check the quote before it goes in the paper); [V-ABS] abstract only (full text not accessible to us); [S] secondary source; [U] unverified / memory. "Inference" marks the literature search's own reasoning.
Compiled 2026-09-28/29 by the literature search. Sources: PubMed E-utilities abstracts, PMC full texts (XML), open PDFs (author/repository copies), OSF API, Crossref/DOI metadata. Blocked: Springer/APA/Elsevier/OUP PDFs (bot challenges), so several classic papers are [V-ABS] or [S].

## 0. Bottom line for the paper (read first)

0. **Prior art on measuring holds from broadcasts exists.** Otsuka et al. (2017) measured set-onset-to-gun intervals for 83 championship 100 m races (OG 2012; WCH 2011/2013/2015) from TV audio waveforms, by eye, at 10 ms resolution: mean 1.780 s, SD 0.158 s [V-FT]. Haugen et al. (2013) used television recordings of 267 heats [V-ABS]. An *automated, validated* pipeline is still new as far as these searches show, but the paper must cite both and must not claim to be the first to measure holds from broadcasts.
1. **The sign conflict has candidate explanations in FP theory.** Otsuka et al. (2017) ran a lab variable-FP design: five FPs (1.465-2.096 s), each presented once per athlete in random order, 20% catch trials, 120 dB go tone [V-FT]; RT fell from 156 to 117 ms, a hazard-like pattern. Championship holds are unimodal (mean 1.780 s, SD 0.158 s; Otsuka's broadcast sample) with a soft right tail and occasional aborted starts. For such distributions the accounts diverge: the blurred reciprocal-PDF account (Grabenhorst 2019, 2021, 2025, 2026) predicts **fastest RT slightly before the typical hold and slower RT for unusually long holds** (a positive slope above ~1.7 s, the same sign as Haugen's r = +0.16/+0.17); the memory account (fMTP) predicts speeding up to the modal hold and then a **plateau**; objective and Weber-blurred hazard predict a **negative** slope (continued speeding), unless aborts ("catch" starts) are frequent enough to make the hazard peak and fall (section "Model predictions"). Closest lab analogue: the fMTP Gaussian-FP experiment at 1.3/1.95/2.6 s found RT 361.8 -> 330.4 -> 328.9 ms, i.e., speeding to the modal FP then a plateau (BF = 94.7 for no FP2-FP3 difference) [V-FT supplement, OSF eu7sd]. But Janssen & Shadlen's monkeys (unimodal 0.5-2 s, no catch trials) sped up monotonically [V-FT], and so did Otsuka's sprinters [V-FT]. Which regime holds at championships is an empirical question our data can answer, which makes championship data a field test of hazard vs PDF vs memory accounts at elite level with real stakes.
2. **Selection matters.** False starts are removed from RT data. If anticipations rise with elapsed hold time (PVT: false starts +5.71 percentage points as the RSI grows 2 -> 10 s, Tucker 2009 [V-FT*]; intense go signals: false starts "increased as the IS was presented later", 26% in predictable blocks vs < 5% unpredictable, Leow 2018 [V-FT]), valid RTs at long holds are left-truncated, which biases the RT-hold slope upward. Any model must treat sub-0.100 s responses and DQs as censored/competing outcomes (race models: Kornblum 1973; Tiefenau 2006; Hsu 2005).
3. **Force-based detection interacts with preparation.** Response force is *lower* when temporal preparation is high (Mattes & Ulrich 1997) and is higher at short FPs (Van der Lubbe 2004). Start systems trigger on force thresholds, so the measured "RT" can depend on FP through force as well as timing.
4. **Loud go signals and high readiness produce very short latencies.** StartReact latencies of about 70 ms regardless of FP (Cressman 2006 [V-ABS]), "< 80 ms" (Carlsen & Maslovat 2026 [V-ABS]; Carlsen & Mackinnon 2010 [V-FT]); in sprint starts with a fixed 3 s foreperiod a 120 dB go signal gave 120 ms vs 138 ms at 80 dB, and startle cut RT by 18 ms (Brown 2008 [V-FT]). Legitimacy of sub-0.100 s responses should be discussed as a function of preparation level, which is itself a function of hold time.
5. **Individual history counts.** Sequential and long-term memory effects (Los 2017; Mattiesing 2017, one week; Kruijne 2022) imply an athlete's recent experienced holds (previous round, same starter) should predict RT beyond the current hold; the warning stimulus acts as a retrieval cue (Los 2021), so starter identity/voice may matter.

## 1. Classic phenomena: fixed vs variable FP

- **Woodrow H (1914).** The measurement of attention. *Psychological Monographs* 17(5):i-158. doi:10.1037/h0093087. [S via Los et al. 2014; Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026] First demonstration that a warning signal alters readiness; reported both the constant-FP increase of RT with FP and irregular (variable) FP effects; constant-FP RT-FP function reaches its nadir by ~300 ms, then increases slowly until it levels off toward ~20 s (as summarized by Los et al. 2014).
- **Karlin L (1959).** Reaction time as a function of foreperiod duration and variability. *J Exp Psychol* 58(2):185-191. doi:10.1037/h0049152. [S] Classic demonstration of opposite RT-FP slopes for constant vs variable FPs and of sequential effects; if the FP range is small relative to its mean, the RT-FP function flattens and sequential effects deviate from the typical pattern (Los 2014 citing Karlin 1959; Steinborn et al. 2008).
- **Drazin DH (1961).** Effects of foreperiod, foreperiod variability, and probability of stimulus occurrence on simple reaction time. *J Exp Psychol* 62(1):43-50. doi:10.1037/h0046860. [S via Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026] RTs are shortest at the longest FP "regardless of their absolute duration"; also manipulated stimulus-occurrence probability (catch trials) and showed sequential effects.
- **Bertelson P, Tisseyre F (1968).** The time-course of preparation with regular and irregular foreperiods. *QJEP* 20(3):297-300. doi:10.1080/14640746808400165. [V-ABS] FPs 0-300 ms after a click; the time course of preparation was similar with predictable and unpredictable FPs: "a shift from preparation to reaction can occur at any time".
- **Niemi P, Näätänen R (1981).** Foreperiod and simple reaction time. *Psychol Bull* 89(1):133-162. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.89.1.133. [S; metadata V] The canonical review (712 citations in Semantic Scholar). Summarized in later reviews: constant FP -> RT increases with FP (time uncertainty); variable FP -> RT decreases with FP (conditional probability); asymmetric sequential effects; no single optimal FP.
- **Los SA, Kruijne W, Meeter M (2014)** (full text read) summarize the "family" of FP phenomena [V-FT, PMC4168672]: (A) with increasing FP, "mean RT increases in the constant-FP paradigm, but decreases in the variable-FP paradigm, typically according to an exponential decay function", converging at the longest FP; (B) as the relative frequency of short FPs increases, the variable-FP RT-FP function "gradually lies down and becomes approximately flat in the case of an exponential FP-distribution" (Näätänen 1971; Trillenberg 2000; Vangkilde 2013); (C) asymmetric sequential effect: RT longer to the extent that FP(n-1) is longer than FP(n); the dependence vanishes for the longest FP(n). Deviations for small FP ranges "probably reflect the inaccuracy of our mental timekeeping system, which has been shown to scale with the duration of FP following a Weber fraction".
- **Bausenhart KM, Ulrich R (2026).** What is prepared in temporal preparation? A review and a historical appreciation. *Front Psychol* 17:1768431. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1768431. PMC13275726. [V-FT] Most current comprehensive review. Key points: in constant FPs, "Longer delays induce greater temporal uncertainty"; very short FPs (up to ~200-400 ms) give a U-shape; variable-FP RTs are longer than constant-FP RTs overall and decrease with FP; the variable-FP function is best described as nonlinear exponential decay (citing Houshmand Chatroudi 2024); sequential effects hold for long (1,200-3,600 ms) and short (200-600 ms) FP sets and for higher-order sequences; hazard can be tracked in SMA EEG; notes the reciprocal-PDF account (Grabenhorst) as "mathematically simpler and more stable than hazard rate"; explains the asymmetry by temporal re-orienting after an expected short FP passes. Also reviews response force, EMG (preparation mainly shortens pre-motor time: e.g., 3 ms motor-time vs 20 ms FP effect on RT, Tandonnet 2003), reflexes (preparatory inhibition in the responding limb prevents premature responding), TMS (MEP suppression before the go signal).
- **Houshmand Chatroudi A, Mioni G, Yotsumoto Y (2024).** On the nonlinearity of the foreperiod effect. *Sci Rep* 14:2780. doi:10.1038/s41598-024-53347-y. PMC10837441. Erratum: *Sci Rep* 15:9608 (2025), doi:10.1038/s41598-025-94097-9. [V-FT] Variable FP task, 7 FPs (480, 720, ..., 1920 ms; uniform); n = 109 (69 lab + 40 online, Padova). Linear and log-linear models fit poorly; a 3-parameter exponential decay RT = a·exp(-b·FP) + c fits best (a = range of RT modulation, b = decay rate, c = asymptote). Motivated by Weber-blurred hazard (illustration with φ = 0.49). Notes that practice can reduce the Weber ratio, making the function closer to linear. **Data + scripts: OSF https://osf.io/km27b/** (dataset lead). Implication: use nonlinear (exponential/saturating) FP terms, not raw linear FP, when modelling sprint RT.
- **Fixed-FP time-uncertainty mechanism** [V-FT of Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026]: preparation "spreads over a 'range of expected moments' (Treisman 1964)" that grows roughly in proportion to FP (scalar property; Gibbon 1977), so longer fixed FPs give less precise preparation and longer RT; synchronization key-presses become more variable with longer FPs (Näätänen et al. 1974); countdowns reduce RT.
- **Fukushi T, Ohtsuki T (2004).** Independence of reaction time and response force control during isometric leg extension. *J Sports Sci* 22(4):373-382. doi:10.1080/02640410310001641601. [V-ABS] Lower-limb analogue: maximal isometric one-leg extension to an auditory stimulus after one of 13 FPs (0.5-10 s). Irregular (variable) condition: shorter FP -> longer RT; regular (constant) condition: shorter FP -> shorter RT. Peak force affected only by regularity, idiosyncratically across participants: RT and force are controlled independently. Useful because block starts are leg-extension actions.

## 2. Conditional probability / hazard accounts (and their neural correlates)

Classic behavioural foundation:
- **Elithorn A, Lawrence C (1955).** Central inhibition: some refractory observations. *QJEP* 7(3):116-127. doi:10.1080/17470215508416684. [V-ABS; hazard attribution S] Early interpretation of RT in terms of "the internal anticipatory set"; cited by Bausenhart & Ulrich (2026) among the origins of the conditional-probability (expectancy) account.
- **Näätänen R (1970).** The diminishing time-uncertainty with the lapse of time after the warning signal in reaction-time experiments with varying fore-periods. *Acta Psychol* 34:399-419. doi:10.1016/0001-6918(70)90035-1. [S; SS tldr] The passage of time after the warning signal is "information-generating": with randomized FPs, uncertainty about *when* diminishes as time elapses, explaining the downward variable-FP function.
- **Näätänen R (1971).** Non-aging fore-periods and simple reaction time. *Acta Psychol* 35(4):316-327. doi:10.1016/0001-6918(71)90040-0. [S via Los 2014, Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026] With a non-aging (exponential, constant-hazard) FP distribution the RT-FP function is approximately flat. Also the source of the **Motor Readiness Model**: excitatory minus inhibitory commands define motor readiness; a response is emitted when readiness crosses a fixed "motor action limit"; readiness is regulated to approach but not cross it prematurely; "it can also account for premature responses if motor readiness is noisy and approaches the motor limit closely" (Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026, V-FT). Directly maps onto false starts.
- **Nickerson RS, Burnham DW (1969).** Response times with nonaging foreperiods. *J Exp Psychol* 79(3, Pt.1):452-457. doi:10.1037/h0026889. [S] Non-aging FPs weaken/abolish the variable-FP effect (cited with Näätänen 1970/1971 in Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026).
- **Thomas EAC (1974).** The selectivity of preparation. *Psychol Rev* 81(5):442-464. doi:10.1037/h0036945. [U content; metadata V] Formal expectancy/preparation model (preparation allocated over time by conditional probability).
- **Luce RD (1986).** *Response Times: Their Role in Inferring Elementary Mental Organization.* Oxford University Press (Oxford Psychology Series 8). [S] Standard reference for hazard functions of RT and FP distributions (ch. 4) and for anticipations and fast-guess contamination.
- **Trillenberg P, Verleger R, Wascher E, Wauschkuhn B, Wessel K (2000).** CNV and temporal uncertainty with 'ageing' and 'non-ageing' S1-S2 intervals. *Clin Neurophysiol* 111(7):1216-1226. doi:10.1016/S1388-2457(00)00274-1. [V-ABS] **FPs of 1.3-2.6 s (three values), with ageing, non-ageing and Gaussian distributions in separate blocks** — almost exactly the championship hold range. RTs "determined by the probability of the imperative stimulus at the given length of the foreperiod"; CNV amplitude tracked the a posteriori probability too. The closest lab analogue to starter holds (includes a Gaussian condition).
- Generic definition used throughout (Grabenhorst 2019, V-FT): h(t) = f(t) / (1 - F(t)); with catch trials the CDF ceiling is 1 - P(catch), which stabilizes h(t) at the right tail.

- **Janssen P, Shadlen MN (2005).** A representation of the hazard rate of elapsed time in macaque area LIP. *Nat Neurosci* 8(2):234-241. doi:10.1038/nn1386. https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1386 . PMID 15657597. [V-FT, submitted version via KU Leuven Lirias https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/49af7c76-3cc6-43b7-861c-87e62069727d] Design: "In the bimodal time schedule ... the 'go' signal could come either early or late, but not in the interval between 0.75 and 1.75 s, whereas in the unimodal time schedule ... the 'go' times were distributed between 0.5 and 2 s." Model: "Because the brain cannot estimate elapsed time precisely, the mathematical functions are replaced by blurred versions, which we term subjective hazard rates or anticipation functions"; "The Weber fraction was fixed (equation (4), φ = 0.26)". Shape: "When 'go' times are drawn from the unimodal distribution, the anticipation function mainly increases with waiting time"; bimodal: "triphasic: it rises, falls and rises again". Behaviour: "In 'unimodal schedule' blocks, reaction time decreased with longer waiting times"; partial r(RT, unimodal anticipation) = -0.24; bimodal (monkey J) -0.36; RT change per unit anticipation from -9.7 to -69.8 ms (Table 1). Note: no catch trials and a bounded range, so the end of the unimodal range is certain; this differs from the athletics case.
- **Cui X, Stetson C, Montague PR, Eagleman DM (2009).** Ready...go: Amplitude of the fMRI signal encodes expectation of cue arrival time. *PLoS Biol* 7(8):e1000167. doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1000167. PMC2711330. [V-ABS] Explicitly framed as "when a sprinter waits for the starting pistol". Little fMRI activity during the wait; a large post-go signal (most prominently SMA) whose amplitude encodes the *cumulative hazard* of the learned waiting-time distribution; the dependence vanishes in a "countdown" (known-time) condition; present in no-go trials; modality-independent (auditory = visual). Good framing citation for the introduction.
- **Coull JT (2009).** Neural substrates of mounting temporal expectation. *PLoS Biol* 7(8):e1000166. doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1000166. PMC2711332. [V-ABS] Primer accompanying Cui et al.
- **Coull JT, Cotti J, Vidal F (2016).** Differential roles for parietal and frontal cortices in fixed versus evolving temporal expectations: Dissociating prior from posterior temporal probabilities with fMRI. *NeuroImage* 141:40-51. doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2016.07.036. PMID 27431757. [V-ABS] Four FPs, cued (predictable) vs neutral (variable). RTs faster with temporal cues; RTs got faster with FP **only in the neutral (variable) condition** ("evolving temporal predictability of the hazard function"). Left inferior parietal cortex tracks hazard; right inferior frontal cortex also (ROI).
- **Nobre A, Correa A, Coull J (2007).** The hazards of time. *Curr Opin Neurobiol* 17(4):465-470. doi:10.1016/j.conb.2007.07.006. [V-ABS] Review of temporal-expectation effects on perception and action.
- **Nobre AC, van Ede F (2018).** Anticipated moments: temporal structure in attention. *Nat Rev Neurosci* 19(1):34-48. doi:10.1038/nrn.2017.141. [V-ABS] Review of temporal attention: hazard-based, cue-based, rhythm-based and sequence-based temporal expectations.
- **Schoffelen JM, Oostenveld R, Fries P (2005).** Neuronal coherence as a mechanism of effective corticospinal interaction. *Science* 308(5718):111-113. doi:10.1126/science.1107027. [V-ABS] In a simple RT task, "modulations of subjects' readiness to respond" were closely correlated with gamma-band (40-70 Hz) corticospinal coherence; coherence "may contribute to an effective corticospinal interaction and shortened reaction times". (Readiness was manipulated via the go-cue probability over a variable wait [S/U detail, not checked in full text].)
- **Bestmann S, Harrison LM, Blankenburg F, Mars RB, Haggard P, Friston KJ, Rothwell JC (2008).** Influence of uncertainty and surprise on human corticospinal excitability during preparation for action. *Curr Biol* 18(10):775-780. doi:10.1016/j.cub.2008.04.051. PMC2387198. [V-ABS] TMS read-out of corticospinal excitability (CSE) in an instructed-delay task; CSE increases with low entropy and low surprise; "commensurate effects" in RT. Caveat: manipulation is cue validity (*what*), not *when*; cite as "motor output is biased by learned contextual probabilities", not as hazard evidence.
- **Tsunoda Y, Kakei S (2008).** Reaction time changes with the hazard rate for a behaviorally relevant event when monkeys perform a delayed wrist movement task. *Neurosci Lett* 433(2):152-157. doi:10.1016/j.neulet.2007.12.063. [V-ABS] Uniform FP 1-2 s: RT inversely related to hazard; LATER-model analysis: RT distributions "swiveled" on reciprobit plots as FP increased (change in anticipation, i.e., starting level/prior, not rate). A 0.9-1.5 s rectangular distribution (higher hazard peaks) produced a larger RT decrease. Directly relevant FP range (about the sprint hold range) and a distributional model (LATER) usable for sub-0.100 s tail predictions.
- **Bueti D, Bahrami B, Walsh V, Rees G (2010).** Encoding of temporal probabilities in the human brain. *J Neurosci* 30(12):4343-4352. doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2254-09.2010. PMC6634482. [V-ABS] Temporal probability of expected visual events encoded by a wide network including earliest visual cortex; activity changes dynamically with temporal expectation.
- **Herbst SK, Fiedler L, Obleser J (2018).** Tracking temporal hazard in the human electroencephalogram using a forward encoding model. *eNeuro* 5(2):ENEURO.0017-18.2018. doi:10.1523/ENEURO.0017-18.2018. PMC5938715. [V-ABS] Implicit monotonic vs peaked hazard (peak at intermediate FP); EEG forward-encoding models distinguish conditions; tracking best reconstructed from SMA.

## 3. Alternatives to (objective) hazard: PDF accounts and Bayesian updating

- **Grabenhorst M, Michalareas G, Maloney LT, Poeppel D (2019).** The anticipation of events in time. *Nat Commun* 10:5802. doi:10.1038/s41467-019-13849-0. PMC6925136. [V-ABS] Presented event PDFs in vision, audition and somatosensation. Claim: "perceptual systems use the reciprocal PDF and not the HR to model event probability density"; temporal uncertainty "does not necessarily grow with elapsed time but can also diminish, depending on the event PDF". Proposes the reciprocal (blurred) PDF as a "simpler and more stable computation than HR".
- **Grabenhorst M, Maloney LT, Poeppel D, Michalareas G (2021).** Two sources of uncertainty independently modulate temporal expectancy. *PNAS* 118(16):e2019342118. doi:10.1073/pnas.2019342118. PMC8072397. [V-ABS; methods V-FT* via fetch tool on the PMC page] Separates *whether* (discrete occurrence probability; catch trials) from *when* (continuous PDF). The occurrence-probability effect "is highly dynamic and monotonically increases across time", independent of the *when* PDF; "the HR fails to account for behavior"; proposes a PDF-based expectancy model; vision and audition. Full-text details (fetch-tool quotes; re-check before quoting in the paper): "All distributions were offset by 0.4 s, resulting in a range of go times from 0.4 to 1.43 s for uniform, exponential, and flipped exponential conditions and 0.4 to 1.4 s for the Gaussian condition"; catch-trial probability 0, 0.2 or 0.4 by block; "A total of 24 human participants"; "models based on the reciprocal probability or its log outperform models employing the HR"; anonymized RT data deposited in Edmond (MPG). Includes a **Gaussian** go-time condition.
- **Grabenhorst M, Poeppel D, Michalareas G (2026).** The anticipation of imminent events is time-scale invariant. *PNAS* 123(2):e2518982123. doi:10.1073/pnas.2518982123. PMC12799109. [V-ABS] NEW (Jan 2026). RT "scales with the event distribution" irrespective of time span; PDF estimation is invariant across temporal scales, and "the precision of anticipation is also scale invariant which contradicts Weber's law"; vision and audition. Implication: a PDF model fitted to one starter's hold distribution should transfer across holds of different absolute length; Weber-type blur assumptions should be treated as a fitted parameter, not a fixed constant.
- Design details of Grabenhorst 2019 [V-FT]: "set-go" task; go times 0.4-1.4 s drawn from a truncated exponential or its flipped version (symmetric PDFs, opposite HR slopes); **9.09% catch trials** (30/330) to stop the brain from treating the end of the range as certain; 24 subjects, ~3500 RTs each; temporal blurring σ = φ·t with **φ = 0.21**; also "probabilistic blurring" where uncertainty is inversely related to event probability. The popular "temporally-blurred, mirrored HR" model "failed to fit the data adequately". Data: available on request only.
- Design details of Grabenhorst 2026 [V-FT]: 13 participants; truncated uniform, exponential and flipped-exponential go-time distributions over spans of 1, 1.7 and 2.4 s (0.4-1.4, 0.4-2.1, 0.4-2.8 s); 9.09% catch trials; φ = 0.21. In the **uniform** case RT curves "approximated a U-shaped pattern" (fastest mid-range), captured by probabilistic blurring; RT IQR ~0.07 s in every span (short 0.074 ± 0.015 s, medium 0.071 ± 0.020 s, long 0.074 ± 0.014 s), i.e., no Weber growth of RT spread. **Data: Edmond (MPG) doi:10.17617/3.MEAGMS** (dataset lead; go times 0.4-2.8 s overlap sprint holds).
- **Grabenhorst M, Poeppel D, Michalareas G (2025).** Neural signatures of temporal anticipation in human cortex represent event probability density. *Nat Commun* 16:2602. doi:10.1038/s41467-025-57813-7. [V-ABS] MEG; RT "approximates the event probability density function, but not hazard rate"; pre-cue alpha/beta activity in three parieto-temporal and sensorimotor areas encodes the event PDF and predicts RT.
- **Huang YT, Chao ZC (2025).** Human brain integrates both unconditional and conditional timing statistics to guide expectation and behavior. *PLoS Biol* 23(10):e3003459. doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.3003459. [V-ABS] Paired-trial "foreperiod sequence" paradigm: prediction of FP2 can use its unconditional distribution or its distribution conditional on FP1; the best RT model integrates both (as hazard functions) with mutual modulation; EEG sources: unconditional (posterior), conditional (anterior), integration (right posterior cingulate). Relevance: an athlete may combine the starter's overall hold distribution with the hold of the immediately preceding start (e.g., after a recall).
- **Oswal A, Ogden M, Carpenter RHS (2007).** The time course of stimulus expectation in a saccadic decision task. *J Neurophysiol* 97(4):2722-2730. doi:10.1152/jn.01238.2006. [V-ABS] LATER model explains how latency (mean and full distribution) varies with FP via expectation (prior); expectancy is "subject to a process of low-pass filtering, analogous to the spatial blur" (temporal blur). Distributional model usable for tail (sub-0.100 s) predictions. See also Carpenter RHS, Williams MLL (1995) Neural computation of log likelihood in control of saccadic eye movements, *Nature* 377:59-62, doi:10.1038/377059a0 [metadata V].
- **Visalli A, Capizzi M, Ambrosini E, Mazzonetto I, Vallesi A (2019).** Bayesian modeling of temporal expectations in the human brain. *NeuroImage* 202:116097. doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2019.116097. [V-ABS] Ideal-Bayesian-observer model of how prior FP expectations are *updated*; updating vs surprise map onto fronto-parietal vs cingulo-opercular networks.
- **Visalli A, Capizzi M, Ambrosini E, Kopp B, Vallesi A (2021).** Electroencephalographic correlates of temporal Bayesian belief updating and surprise. *NeuroImage* 231:117867. doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.117867. [V-ABS] 26 participants, temporal FP task; surprise modulates the P3b, updating a later sustained P3b-like wave. **Data and scripts on OSF: https://osf.io/ckqa5/** (dataset lead).
- **Visalli A, Capizzi M, Ambrosini E, Kopp B, Vallesi A (2023).** P3-like signatures of temporal predictions: a computational EEG study. *Exp Brain Res* 241(7):1919-1930. doi:10.1007/s00221-023-06656-z. [V-ABS] Incidental learning of temporal probabilities in a simple RT task; ideal Bayesian observer. **Data and scripts on OSF: osf.io/sdy8j/** (dataset lead).
- Relevance for sprint: a Bayesian observer that learns a *starter-specific* hold distribution across heats (prior) and updates after each start is a direct, citable model class (Visalli/Vallesi) for the "starter-specific hazard" novelty angle.

## 4. Sequential effects and memory / trace-conditioning accounts

- **Los SA, van den Heuvel CE (2001).** Intentional and unintentional contributions to nonspecific preparation during reaction time foreperiods. *JEP:HPP* 27(2):370-386. doi:10.1037/0096-1523.27.2.370. [V-ABS] Trace-conditioning view (the warning stimulus acts as a CS that unintentionally elicits a response tendency at the previously critical moment) accounts for FP-duration and FP-variability effects at least as well as the strategic view; 3 experiments dissociate unintentional from intentional preparation.
- **Los SA, Knol DL, Boers RM (2001).** The foreperiod effect revisited: conditioning as a basis for nonspecific preparation. *Acta Psychol* 106(1-2):121-145. doi:10.1016/S0001-6918(00)00029-9. [V-ABS] Adapted Machado's (1997) formal conditioning model; fitted to 9 participants; moderate variance explained but reproduced key features.
- **Los SA, Agter F (2005).** Reweighting sequential effects across different distributions of foreperiods. *Percept Psychophys* 67(7):1161-1170. doi:10.3758/BF03193549. [V-ABS] Within-block FP variation gives a downward-sloping FP-RT function; **slope depends on the FP distribution ("the more negative the skewness, the steeper the slope")** and on the previous FP ("the longer the FP on the preceding trial, the steeper the slope"). Uniform, exponential and peaked distributions compared; reweighting sequential effects explained little, suggesting "a dominant role of temporal orienting strategies". KEY for sprint: starter hold distributions are peaked, so the FP-RT slope should be distribution-dependent.
- **Van der Lubbe RHJ, Los SA, Jaśkowski P, Verleger R (2004).** Being prepared on time: on the importance of the previous foreperiod to current preparation, as reflected in speed, force and preparation-related brain potentials. *Acta Psychol* 116(3):245-262. doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2004.03.003. [V-ABS] FPs 500/1500/2500 ms, two-choice task. The RT sequential effect (slower when current FP shorter than previous) is mirrored in CNV and LRP; **response force increased with brief current FPs but did not depend on the previous FP**. Relevance: start systems detect *force*; force is higher at short FPs (see Mattes & Ulrich 1997, section 5).
- **Steinborn MB, Rolke B, Bratzke D, Ulrich R (2009).** Dynamic adjustment of temporal preparation: shifting warning signal modality attenuates the sequential foreperiod effect. *Acta Psychol* 132(1):40-47. doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2009.06.002. [V-ABS] Changing warning-signal modality between trials attenuates the asymmetry of the sequential FP effect, consistent with the WS acting as a conditioned/retrieval cue. Relevance: in athletics the warning signal ("set") is spoken by a different starter each session and may differ in voice, loudness and cadence; retrieval cues could be starter-specific.
- **Steinborn MB, Rolke B, Bratzke D, Ulrich R (2008).** Sequential effects within a short foreperiod context: Evidence for the conditioning account of temporal preparation. *Acta Psychol* 129(2):297-307. doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2008.08.005. [metadata V via Crossref; content S] Sequential effects occur within short FP sets (200-600 ms) (Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026 [V-FT]) and are diminished for dense sets such as 400/500/600 ms (as cited by Han & Proctor 2022a [V-FT]). Relevance: multi-second holds are a "long-FP context".
- **Los SA, Nieuwenstein J, Bouharab A, Stephens DJ, Meeter M, Kruijne W (2021).** The warning stimulus as retrieval cue: The role of associative memory in temporal preparation. *Cogn Psychol* 125:101378. doi:10.1016/j.cogpsych.2021.101378. [V-ABS] Eight experiments: two S1s paired with short- vs long-dominated FP distributions; only **highly distinct S1s** (e.g., tone vs flash) modified preparation, even without awareness, persisting in transfer. - **Kruijne W, Galli RM, Los SA (2022).** Implicitly learning when to be ready: From instances to categories. *Psychon Bull Rev* 29(2):552-562. doi:10.3758/s13423-021-02004-w. [V-ABS] Category-level (faces vs scenes) cue-FP contingencies generalized to never-seen exemplars implicitly and persisted after explicit instruction; rolling-regression shows gradual build-up.
- **Welhaf MS (2026).** Age-related differences in implicit temporal preparation. *Front Psychol* 17:1734451. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1734451. [V-ABS] Two studies: older adults slower overall but with similar variable-FP and sequential-FP effects to young adults (contrast with Vallesi et al. 2009; section 7).
- **Vallesi A, Shallice T (2007).** Developmental dissociations of preparation over time: deconstructing the variable foreperiod phenomena. *JEP:HPP* 33(6):1377-1388. doi:10.1037/0096-1523.33.6.1377. [V-ABS] Variable FPs 1/3/5 s and 1/2/3 s; **sequential effects present already at 4-5 years, the FP effect emerges years later**, supporting a dual-process account (automatic sequential + strategic/hazard FP effect).
- **Vallesi A, Shallice T, Walsh V (2007).** Role of the prefrontal cortex in the foreperiod effect: TMS evidence for dual mechanisms in temporal preparation. *Cereb Cortex* 17(2):466-474. doi:10.1093/cercor/bhj163. [V-ABS] TMS over right DLPFC reduced the FP effect (simple and choice RT) but **not** sequential effects; rDLPFC lesion patients lack the typical FP effect.

[V-ABS] MTP: during the FP **"inhibition is applied to prevent premature response"**, while a wave of activation occurs at S2; each trial is stored as a memory trace that contributes to later preparation. Accounts for RT-FP functions under different distributions and asymmetric sequential effects. The explicit inhibition-of-premature-response component maps directly onto false-start avoidance.
- **Los SA, Kruijne W, Meeter M (2017).** Hazard versus history: Temporal preparation is driven by past experience. *JEP:HPP* 43(1):78-88. doi:10.1037/xhp0000279. [V-ABS] Groups trained on different FP distributions showed transfer effects in a test phase with the same (explicitly instructed) uniform distribution: "temporal preparation is driven by past experience, not by current hazard".
- **Mattiesing RM, Kruijne W, Meeter M, Los SA (2017).** Timing a week later: The role of long-term memory in temporal preparation. *Psychon Bull Rev* 24(6):1900-1905. doi:10.3758/s13423-017-1270-3. [V-ABS] Effects of an acquisition-phase FP distribution persisted **one week later**, despite full information about the new distribution. Relevance: athletes' expectations may be shaped by long-run exposure to typical holds (home-nation starters, training starts), not only by the current championship starter.

[V-ABS] Non-aging distributions yield unequal sequential effects (larger at shorter FP), consistent with MTP, contrary to repetition priming; FP-RT functions under non-aging distributions resemble fixed-FP functions (increasing), "not predicted by the multiple trace theory".

## 5. Anticipations, false alarms and the 100 ms convention

Models of anticipation (directly usable for a foreperiod-dependent false-start model):
- **Ollman RT, Billington MJ (1972).** The deadline model for simple reaction times. *Cogn Psychol* 3(2):311-336. doi:10.1016/0010-0285(72)90010-2. [S] The subject plans a timed (anticipatory) response for a "deadline" near the longest expected FP but departs from the plan whenever the signal arrives first: the response is emitted at the deadline or at signal detection, whichever comes first. Tested with and without catch trials. Sprint reading: a sprinter with an internal deadline tied to the typical hold will false-start when the actual hold exceeds that deadline.
- **Kornblum S (1973).** Simple reaction time as a race between signal detection and time estimation: A paradigm and model. *Percept Psychophys* 13:108-112 [pages U]. doi:10.3758/BF03207243. [metadata V; content S via Hsu 2005] Race between a time-estimation (anticipatory) process and a signal-detection process; basis for estimating the "cut-off" between true and anticipatory RTs.
- **Hsu YF (2005).** On measuring the minimum detection time: a simple reaction time study in the time estimation paradigm. *Br J Math Stat Psychol* 58(2):259-284. doi:10.1348/000711005X62774. [V-ABS] Extends Kornblum's race model to estimate the "cut-off" separating true from anticipatory RTs: time-estimation latency gamma-distributed, signal latency Weibull-distributed and **shifted by the cut-off**; fits well. A principled, data-driven alternative to a fixed 0.100 s threshold (method template for the paper).
- **Tiefenau A, Neubauer H, von Specht H, Heil P (2006).** Correcting for false alarms in a simple reaction time task. *Brain Res* 1122(1):99-115. doi:10.1016/j.brainres.2006.09.004. [V-ABS] Catch trials measure false-alarm tendencies; a race between a stimulus-controlled process and a false-alarm process predicts the numbers and timing of early and late RTs; proposed as an alternative to truncation.
- **Boulinguez P, Jaffard M, Granjon L, Benraiss A (2008).** Warning signals induce automatic EMG activations and proactive volitional inhibition: evidence from analysis of error distribution in simple RT. *J Neurophysiol* 99(3):1572-1578. doi:10.1152/jn.01198.2007. [V-ABS] EMG detects covert and overt errors: **77% of all EMG-detected errors are responses to the warning signal**; movement triggering must be proactively inhibited during the FP to prevent premature responses. Sprint reading: the "set" command is itself a trigger for premature activation; covert EMG "twitches" are a real phenomenon, not only overt false starts.
- **Näätänen R (1972).** Time uncertainty and occurrence uncertainty of the stimulus in a simple reaction time task. *Acta Psychol* 36(6):492-503. doi:10.1016/0001-6918(72)90029-7. [metadata V; content U] Separates uncertainty about *when* from uncertainty about *whether* (catch trials).
- **Grabenhorst et al. (2021)** (section 3) show the *whether* component (catch-trial probability) acts dynamically, increasing across the FP, independent of the *when* PDF [V-ABS]. In athletics, aborted starts ("stand up" recalls) play the role of catch trials.
- **Correa Á, Lupiáñez J, Tudela P (2006).** The attentional mechanism of temporal orienting: determinants and attributes. *Exp Brain Res* 169(1):58-68. doi:10.1007/s00221-005-0131-x. [V-ABS] 400 vs 1,400 ms cued intervals: at the long interval, temporal-orienting effects appear only when catch trials are present (without catch trials people re-orient to the certain later moment).

Anticipation vs FP, quantitative:
- **Tucker AM, Basner RC, Stern Y, Rakitin BC (2009).** The variable response-stimulus interval effect and sleep deprivation: an unexplored aspect of psychomotor vigilance task performance. *Sleep* 32(10):1393-1395. doi:10.1093/sleep/32.10.1393. PMC2753816. [V-ABS; details V-FT*] PVT, RSI random 2-10 s; 84 participants (68 sleep-deprived 54 h, 16 controls). **Mean RT fell by 69.31 ± 10.70 ms as RSI rose from 2 to 10 s**; **%false starts increased by 5.71 (percentage points)** across the same range while lapses decreased 6.65; no interaction with sleep deprivation (preparation over seconds is distinct from vigilance over minutes). False starts defined as responses before the stimulus or RT < 100 ms (< 6% of trials). Key anchor: longer waits -> faster valid RTs AND more premature responses.
- **Basner M, Dinges DF (2011).** Maximizing sensitivity of the psychomotor vigilance test (PVT) to sleep loss. *Sleep* 34(5):581-591. doi:10.1093/sleep/34.5.581. PMC3079937. [V-ABS; definition V-FT*] Standard PVT metrics: **false starts = "Responses without a stimulus or response times < 100 ms"**; ISI random 2-10 s. The paper does not justify the 100 ms value (fetch summary). Cross-domain parallel: the PVT and World Athletics use the same 100 ms convention for "false start".
- **Welhaf (2026)** (section 4) PVT: variable-FP effect 433 vs 397 ms (short vs long FP); sequential effect b ≈ -9 ms; argues continuous FP distributions (as in the PVT, and as with starter holds) weaken detectable sequential effects.

The 100 ms convention and minimum RT:
- **Whelan R (2008).** Effective analysis of reaction time data. *Psychol Rec* 58(3):475-482. doi:10.1007/BF03395630. [S via search snippet] "Very fast reaction times are normally eliminated by using a cutoff of between 100 ms and 200 ms"; recommends distributional analysis (ex-Gaussian etc.) over means. The lab convention is a *range* chosen per task, not a physiological constant.
- Luce (1986) [S] is the usual citation for fastest auditory simple RTs near 100 ms and for fast-guess contamination; exact figures not verified here.
- Startle-triggered prepared movements break the floor (section 6): ~70 ms (Cressman 2006), "< 80 ms" (Carlsen & Maslovat 2026), "almost halved" latencies, some below the calculated minimum cortical processing time (Valls-Solé 1999).

Response force and temporal preparation (matters for force-threshold start systems):
- **Mattes S, Ulrich R (1997).** Response force is sensitive to the temporal uncertainty of response stimuli. *Percept Psychophys* 59(7):1089-1097. doi:10.3758/BF03205523. [V-ABS; design details S = V-FT of Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026] FPs 500/1,750/3,000 ms, constant vs variable. With visual signals, force **decreased with FP in the variable condition and increased in the constant condition**: responses are less forceful when timing is predictable (auditory warning: less sensitive). Explained by an extension of Näätänen's motor-readiness model: high readiness -> small activation increment -> softer response; low readiness -> larger boost -> overshoot, more force.
- **Jaśkowski P, Verleger R, Wascher E (1994).** Response force and reaction time in a simple reaction task under time pressure. *Z Psychol* 202:405-413. [U; no DOI found] Force-RT relations under time pressure.
- **Van der Lubbe et al. (2004)** (section 4): force higher after brief current FPs, independent of previous FP [V-ABS].
- Implication (inference, not verified in sprint data): if start-block force traces are softer/slower-rising when athletes are highly prepared (long, expected holds), a fixed force threshold would register threshold crossing later, adding a preparation-dependent measurement lag to official RT. Worth flagging to the RT/measurement pipelines.

## 6. StartReact / startle and loud go signals

- **Valls-Solé J, Rothwell JC, Goulart F, Cossu G, Muñoz E (1999).** Patterned ballistic movements triggered by a startle in healthy humans. *J Physiol* 516(3):931-938. doi:10.1111/j.1469-7793.1999.0931u.x. PMC2269293. [V-ABS; numbers V-FT* via fetch tool on the PMC page] 14 volunteers; wrist movements or rising onto tiptoe to a visual go; a very loud acoustic stimulus with the go "almost halved the latency" without changing the EMG pattern; in some subjects RTs were "shorter than the calculated minimum time for processing of sensory information at the cerebral cortex"; most reported the very rapid responses were not willed. Leg (tiptoe) movements included. Full-text numbers (fetch-tool quotes): wrist control 203.6 ± 74.5 vs startle 104.3 ± 17.6 ms; rising on tiptoe control 237.6 ± 98.0 vs startle 122.6 ± 25.3 ms; "The sound produced had an intensity of 130 dB SPL, measured at a distance of 1 m from the source"; with ~20 ms efferent conduction, only ~10 ms would remain for cortical processing "for the individual with the shortest Ag1 latency in our study (65 ms)".
- **Carlsen AN, Chua R, Inglis JT, Sanderson DJ, Franks IM (2004).** Prepared movements are elicited early by startle. *J Mot Behav* 36(3):253-264. doi:10.3200/JMBR.36.3.253-264. [V-ABS] 124 dB startle replaced the go stimulus on some trials; the intended response was produced at significantly shorter latency with unchanged kinematics/EMG.
- **Cressman EK, Carlsen AN, Chua R, Franks IM (2006).** Temporal uncertainty does not affect response latencies of movements produced during startle reactions. *Exp Brain Res* 171(2):278-282. doi:10.1007/s00221-006-0459-x. [V-ABS] FPs 2,500-5,500 ms (1,000 ms steps); when startled (124 dB vs 80 dB control), the intended movement started at **~70 ms regardless of FP**; the movement was prepared within 2.5 s of the warning and did not decay for at least 3 s. Relevance: in the sprint hold range, a fully prepared action can be released by a loud sound at latencies well below 100 ms (EMG/kinematic onset, not force-threshold RT).
- **Carlsen AN, Maslovat D, Lam MY, Chua R, Franks IM (2011).** Considerations for the use of a startling acoustic stimulus in studies of motor preparation in humans. *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 35(3):366-376. doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2010.04.009. [V-ABS] Review of startle-as-probe methods (> 120 dB), parameters and confounds.
- **Carlsen AN, Maslovat D (2026).** What is the StartReact effect? *Acta Physiol* 242(6):e70235. doi:10.1111/apha.70235. [V-ABS] NEW. Startle during simple RT "results in the rapid initiation of a prepared response at extremely short latencies (< 80 ms)"; proposes the definition "the early and involuntary triggering of a prepared movement in the presence of a startle reflex"; recommends classifying trials by sternocleidomastoid startle activity; discusses "lowered response preparation levels".
- **Carlsen AN, Mackinnon CD (2010).** Motor preparation is modulated by the resolution of the response timing information. *Brain Res* 1322:38-49. doi:10.1016/j.brainres.2010.01.076. PMC2839417. [V-FT] Variable-FP SRT ("2 – 3 sec. foreperiod"), fixed-FP SRT and two anticipation-timing tasks; 124 dB startle at go -150, -500, -1,500 ms. The time course of pre-programming depended on temporal uncertainty: with low temporal resolution, participants "plan the movement well in advance in accordance with the anticipated probability of onset of the cue". Full-text results: "The intended movement was released early in about 60% of RT (i.e. Variable and Fixed foreperiod condition) trials when a startling acoustic stimulus (SAS) was presented 1500 ms prior to the 'go' signal. This percentage grew to about 90% at 500 ms and 150 ms prior"; at -1500 ms: Variable 57.5%, Fixed 68.1% vs Countdown 10%, Clock 0%; startle-released EMG onset 92.5 ms vs 195.4 ms in control trials; intro: startle can release the planned movement "at latencies markedly shorter (< 80 ms)". I.e., in RT tasks with 2-3 s foreperiods the response is largely prepared ~1.5 s before the expected go, so preparation level (and startle-release susceptibility) is shaped by expected timing. Sprint reading: athletes are probably near full preparation for most of a 1.3-2.2 s hold, which makes premature release by extraneous sounds plausible.
- **Smith V, Maslovat D, Drummond NM, Carlsen AN (2019).** A timeline of motor preparatory state prior to response initiation: Evidence from startle. *Neuroscience* 397:80-93. doi:10.1016/j.neuroscience.2018.11.020. [V-ABS] 500 ms fixed FP; proportion of startle trials releasing the prepared response increased until 100 ms before go: preparation "can take up to 300-400 ms following the WS".
- **Leow LA, Uchida A, Egberts JL, Riek S, Lipp OV, Tresilian J, Marinovic W (2018).** Triggering mechanisms for motor actions: The effects of expectation on reaction times to intense acoustic stimuli. *Neuroscience* 393:226-235. doi:10.1016/j.neuroscience.2018.10.008. [V-FT, accepted manuscript https://eprints.qut.edu.au/206309/1/72506637.pdf] Mini meta-analysis of 10 studies: premotor RT faster in SCM+ than SCM- trials, but **"foreperiod predictability can induce differences in RT that would be of similar size"** to the SCM+/SCM- difference. Temporal expectation and loudness effects are comparable in magnitude and confounded. Full text: SCM+ minus SCM- premotor RT "-16.9 ms 95% CI [-23.7, -10.1]"; own experiment (isometric wrist abduction; torque-sensor onset when rate of force development exceeded 10% of the median; intense IS 114 dBa; IS at 600/1200/1800 ms): "the probability of falsely starting a response increased as the IS was presented later"; "the mean RT in the predictable block for the 3rd IS marker was 103 ms (% of false starts = 26), which would correspond to a premotor RT of about 84 ms ... This estimate would be 96 ms in the unpredictable blocks of trials, when the percentage of false starts was below 5%." RTs < 50 ms were discarded as anticipatory.
- **Marinovic W, Tresilian JR (2016).** Triggering prepared actions by sudden sounds: reassessing the evidence for a single mechanism. *Acta Physiol* 217(1):13-32. doi:10.1111/apha.12627. [V-ABS] StartReact for volitional limb movements likely involves transcortical pathways; "a particular manifestation of the excitatory effects of intense stimulation on the central nervous system".
- **Marinovic W, Tresilian JR, de Rugy A, Sidhu S, Riek S (2014).** Corticospinal modulation induced by sounds depends on action preparedness. *J Physiol* 592(1):153-169. doi:10.1113/jphysiol.2013.254581. [V-ABS] Loud sounds inhibit corticospinal excitability early in preparation but facilitate it close to movement onset: the effect of the go sound depends on readiness.
- **McInnes AN, Lipp OV, Tresilian JR, Vallence AM, Marinovic W (2021).** Premovement inhibition can protect motor actions from interference by response-irrelevant sensory stimulation. *J Physiol* 599(18):4389-4406. doi:10.1113/JP281849. [V-ABS] Foreperiod duration (urgency) manipulated: preparatory corticospinal inhibition absent when urgency high; subcortical circuits progressively inhibited as time to prepare increased; preparatory inhibition may protect prepared actions from interference.
- **Carlsen AN, Santangelo CM, Sadler CM, Maslovat D (2026).** Premovement suppression of corticospinal excitability is modulated by reaction time task requirements. *Eur J Neurosci* 64(4):e70659. doi:10.1111/ejn.70659. [V-ABS] MEP suppression grows as the go signal approaches in SRT, CRT and go/no-go; greatest in SRT and GNG at -50 ms and at the go.
- **Brown AM, Kenwell ZR, Maraj BKV, Collins DF (2008).** "Go" signal intensity influences the sprint start. *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 40(6):1142-1148. doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e31816770e1. [V-FT, author PDF sites.ualberta.ca/~dcollins/Articles/Brown2008.pdf; details in section 7] Athens 2004 Olympics 100/110 m: lane 1 (closest to starter) mean RT 160 ms vs lanes 2-8 175 ± 5 ms; lane 2 < lane 7. Lab (12 untrained, 4 trained; blocks with horizontal force): recorded gunshot at 80/100/120 dB -> RT 138 ± 30, 128 ± 25, 120 ± 20 ms; startle trials 18 ms faster; peak force unaffected. Lab FP "fixed at 3 s ... to eliminate the influence of a variable foreperiod on RT", ~10% catch trials. The only sprint-specific startle study found (searched PubMed "startle AND sprint start", "startle AND (sprinter OR block start OR athletics)" on 2026-09-28: no other hits).
- Synthesis for the paper: sub-0.100 s responses to a loud gun are physiologically possible when preparation is high; preparation is timed by expectation (Carlsen & Mackinnon 2010; Leow 2018). So the probability of a *legitimate* sub-0.100 s force-onset RT should rise with the athlete's readiness at gun time, which the FP literature says depends on hold duration relative to the expected distribution. No study has tested this jointly in sprinting (as of this search).

## 7. Athletes and sports psychology

Sprint-specific:
- **Otsuka M, Kurihara T, Isaka T (2017).** Timing of gun fire influences sprinters' multiple joint reaction times of whole body in block start. *Front Psychol* 8:810. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00810. PMC5435752. [V-FT, publisher PDF] Two findings matter here:
  1. **Broadcast hold measurement (prior art for our method).** "a total of 88 male and female large-scale 100-m races over the last 5 years were recorded from publicly available television broadcasts. The competitions included the 2012 Olympics and the World Championships in 2011, 2013, and 2015 ... The races whose set and start signal could be clearly heard without the voice of the announcer were analyzed, for a total of 83 races. These FP lengths were analyzed by intervals of 10 ms using sound editing software ... Onsets of the starter's set and gun signals were visually determined by sound wave information." Result: "The FP length of the start signal was 1.780 ± 0.017 s (SD: 0.158 s)." (So: manual, waveform-based, 10 ms resolution, 83 races; set-onset to gun-onset.)
  2. **Lab design.** 20 experienced male sprinters; five 5-m dashes, one at each FP (mean −2 SD ... +2 SD: 1.465, 1.622, 1.780, 1.938, 2.096 s), "trial randomly timed between the warning and imperative tones"; warning 70 dB, imperative 120 dB trumpet horn ~30 cm from the ear; "a catch trial in which the go signal was not delivered was used ... The catch trial incidence was 20%". Whole-body RT 156 ± 8, 133 ± 6, 125 ± 4, 129 ± 5, 117 ± 5 ms for 1.465 ... 2.096 s (Table: F = 33.600, p < 0.001). RT onset from force-plate GRF/joint-moment thresholds (baseline −400 to −200 ms, mean ± adjusted SD).
  Interpretation for the sign conflict: each athlete saw each FP only once, so within-experiment learning of the distribution was minimal; expectations came from prior experience (championship holds centred near 1.78 s), plus 20% catch trials. The monotonic speeding (fastest at +2 SD) is hazard-like, not PDF-like. This is the strongest single data point *against* a pure reciprocal-PDF account for sprinters, and it must be addressed in the paper.
- **Brown AM, Kenwell ZR, Maraj BKV, Collins DF (2008).** "Go" signal intensity influences the sprint start. *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 40(6):1142-1148. doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e31816770e1. [V-ABS] Finalists' RT decreases from heats to final; mean RT rises with race length (60 m -> 400 m); juniors do not show the across-round decrease; "RT must be considered a skill dependent upon experience and learning". Supports strategic, context-dependent readiness (relevant to caution vs anticipation strategies).

## 8. Rhythm and entrainment: is the starter's cadence a cue?

- **Breska A, Deouell LY (2017).** Neural mechanisms of rhythm-based temporal prediction: Delta phase-locking reflects temporal predictability but not rhythmic entrainment. *PLoS Biol* 15(2):e2001665. doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.2001665. PMC5302287. [V-ABS] Rhythm-based and memory (interval)-based predictions produce comparable anticipatory EEG effects, but only rhythms cause "obligatory depression of preparation-related premotor brain activity when an on-beat event is omitted", giving larger behavioural costs for violated predictions. Delineates "continuous vigilance, interval-timing-based prediction and rhythm-based prediction".
- **Jones MR, Boltz M (1989).** Dynamic attending and responses to time. *Psychol Rev* 96(3):459-491. doi:10.1037/0033-295X.96.3.459. [U content; metadata V] Dynamic attending theory: attention entrains to temporal regularities.
- **Large EW, Jones MR (1999).** The dynamics of attending: How people track time-varying events. *Psychol Rev* 106(1):119-159. doi:10.1037/0033-295X.106.1.119. [U content; metadata V] Oscillator model of attentional entrainment.
- **Nobre & van Ede (2018)** (section 2) review rhythm-based vs hazard-based vs cue-based expectations [V-ABS].
- **Capizzi M, Attout L, Mioni G, Charras P (2026).** Unifying temporal preparation: The temporal preparation task (TEP-Task). *Behav Res Methods* 58(2):52. doi:10.3758/s13428-025-02908-8. [V-ABS] A 35-min single-session task measuring foreperiod, sequential, temporal-orienting and rhythmic effects together; a candidate instrument if the paper later adds a small self-run athlete test.
- Assessment (inference, not verified): the sprint start contains no periodic sequence within the hold (one warning, one go), so rhythm-based entrainment is unlikely to be the main driver of the hold effect. Two weaker forms are plausible and cheap to check in broadcast audio: (a) a starter's individual "set"-command prosody (duration, loudness) as a retrieval cue for that starter's hold distribution (cf. Los et al. 2021: only highly distinct cues work); (b) a stable ratio between the "on your marks"-to-"set" interval and the "set"-to-gun interval within a starter. Both are exploratory.

## Model predictions for championship sprint RT vs hold

Assumed hold distribution: Gaussian, mean 1.78 s, SD 0.158 s (Otsuka 2017's broadcast measurement of 83 races, 2011-2015 [V-FT]; Haugen 2013 reports a 1.3-2.2 s range [V-ABS]). Curves below are **theoretical illustrations computed by the literature search** (scratchpad scripts `cog/fp_models.py`, `cog/fp_catch.py`; not data, not for the abstract). Temporal blur σ = φ·t with φ = 0.21 (Grabenhorst) or 0.26 (Janssen & Shadlen).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| (i) Fixed-FP time-uncertainty logic (athletes treat the hold as a quasi-fixed ~1.8 s interval) | Monotonic **increase**: preparation spreads over a range of expected moments that widens with elapsed time (Treisman 1964; Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026 [V-FT]). With a deadline strategy (Ollman & Billington 1972 [S]), responses planned for the typical moment | Shortest holds | **Positive** (matches Haugen's r = +0.16/+0.17) | False starts rise when the actual hold exceeds the athlete's internal deadline -> increasing with hold |

What discriminates the accounts in field data: (1) the **location of the fastest hold** relative to the starter's modal hold (PDF: ~0.07 s below the mean; objective hazard with rare aborts: ~0.2 s above; blurred hazard: at the top of the range; fMTP: plateau from the mode); (2) the **sign of the slope above the mode** (PDF +, hazard -, fMTP ≈ 0); (3) whether false-start probability and valid-RT speed move together (hazard) or dissociate (PDF).

Why Haugen (r > 0, field) and Otsuka (monotone speeding, lab) can both be right (hypotheses, not verified):
- **Design.** Otsuka: 5 trials per athlete, one per FP, randomized, 20% catch trials, loud 120 dB go signal near the ear; no chance to learn a peaked distribution in the lab, so elapsed-time hazard-like expectancy dominated [V-FT for design].
- **Selection by false starts.** Championship RT data exclude DQ'd responses. If anticipations rise with hold (ii, iii, iv all predict this), valid RTs at long holds are left-truncated, inflating mean RT and producing r > 0 even if readiness rises. Model with censoring / race models (Kornblum 1973; Hsu 2005; Tiefenau 2006).
- **Caution under DQ rules.** Motor-readiness regulation (Näätänen 1971 via Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026 [V-FT]; MTP inhibition) implies athletes can hold readiness below the action limit; under one-and-done rules, elite athletes may deliberately increase inhibition as the hold extends. Haugen found the relation in 1997-2003 (both sexes) and 2003-2009 (men only) [V-ABS].
- **Force measurement.** Softer responses under high preparation (Mattes & Ulrich 1997) could delay force-threshold crossing at long, expected holds (inference).
- **Physical cost of holding the set position** (untested inference): longer holds may add postural fatigue or tremor, which no lab FP study models.

## Quantitative anchors

Weber fraction (temporal blur, σ = φ·t):
- φ = 0.26 — Janssen & Shadlen 2005 [V-FT: "(A(t), φ = 0.26, equation (4))"; "The Weber fraction was fixed (equation (4), φ = 0.26)"].
- φ = 0.21 — Grabenhorst et al. 2019 [V-FT: "With this value of φ = 0.21 ..."] and 2026 [V-FT: "We used a value of φ=0.21, consistent with previous research"].
- φ = 0.49 — illustration in Houshmand Chatroudi et al. 2024, Fig. 1 [V-FT]; the same paper notes practice can reduce the Weber ratio, making the FP function closer to linear [S: their ref. 35].
- Grabenhorst 2026 found RT spread (IQR ≈ 0.07 s) did **not** grow with the go-time span [V-FT], i.e., Weber growth is not guaranteed; treat φ as a fitted parameter.

Championship hold distribution:
- 1.780 s mean, SD 0.158 s, 83 races (OG 2012; WCH 2011, 2013, 2015), set-onset to gun-onset from broadcast audio waveforms at 10 ms resolution [V-FT, Otsuka 2017]. Range 1.3-2.2 s, heats n = 267 (Haugen 2013) [V-ABS]. CV ≈ 0.09.

FP-effect magnitudes:
- Lab sprint, 5 FPs (1.465-2.096 s): 156 -> 117 ms whole-body RT (-39 ms over 0.63 s; non-monotonic middle: 133, 125, 129) [V-FT, Otsuka 2017].
- Gaussian FP 1.3/1.95/2.6 s (button press): 361.8 / 330.4 / 328.9 ms [V-FT supplement, fMTP OSF].
- Uniform FPs 0.48-1.92 s: 3-parameter exponential decay fits best (n = 109) [V-FT, Houshmand Chatroudi 2024].
- PVT RSI 2 -> 10 s: mean RT -69.31 ± 10.70 ms [V-FT*, Tucker 2009]; short vs long FP 433 vs 397 ms [V-FT, Welhaf 2026].
- Monkeys, unimodal go times 0.5-2 s: RT decreased with waiting time; partial r(RT, anticipation) = -0.24 (unimodal), -0.36 (bimodal, monkey J); RT change per unit anticipation -9.7 to -69.8 ms [V-FT, Janssen & Shadlen 2005 Table 1].
- Leg extension, FPs 0.5-10 s: variable -> shorter FP slower; constant -> shorter FP faster [V-ABS, Fukushi & Ohtsuki 2004].

Sequential effects:
- 24 ms (current FP 400 ms) vs 8 ms (1,400 ms), non-aging distribution, choice RT [V-FT, Han & Proctor 2022a].
- b ≈ -9 ms in PVT-style continuous FP distributions [V-FT, Welhaf 2026].
- Asymmetry: large at short current FP, vanishing at the longest FP [V-FT, Los et al. 2014; V-ABS, Vallesi & Shallice 2007, Van der Lubbe 2004].
- Diminished with dense FP sets (400/500/600 ms) [S, Steinborn et al. 2008 as cited in Han & Proctor 2022a].

Anticipation / false-start rates:
- PVT: false starts (< 100 ms or no stimulus) < 6% of trials; +5.71 percentage points as RSI rises 2 -> 10 s [V-FT*, Tucker 2009].
- Intense go signal: predictable timing -> mean RT 103 ms with 26% false starts; unpredictable -> < 5% false starts [V-FT, Leow 2018 accepted manuscript]. False-start probability "increased as the IS was presented later" [V-FT].
- 77% of EMG-detected errors are responses to the warning signal [V-ABS, Boulinguez 2008].
- Catch-trial rates used: 9.09% (Grabenhorst 2019, 2026), 0/20/40% (Grabenhorst 2021), 20% (Otsuka 2017), ~10% (Brown 2008), 12.5% of experimental trials (fMTP exp2, inferred from SOA counts, U).

Loud go signals / startle:
- StartReact onset ~70 ms regardless of FP 2.5-5.5 s [V-ABS, Cressman 2006]; "< 80 ms" [V-ABS, Carlsen & Maslovat 2026; V-FT, Carlsen & Mackinnon 2010].
- Startle-released EMG onset 92.5 ms vs 195.4 ms control [V-FT, Carlsen & Mackinnon 2010]; release in ~60% of trials when the startle came 1,500 ms before an expected go, ~90% at 500 and 150 ms [V-FT].
- 130 dB startle: wrist 203.6 -> 104.3 ms; rising on tiptoe 237.6 -> 122.6 ms; shortest agonist latency 65 ms [V-FT*, Valls-Solé 1999].
- Sprint start, fixed 3 s FP: 138 / 128 / 120 ms at 80 / 100 / 120 dB; startle -18 ms [V-FT, Brown 2008]. Athens 2004 lane 1 vs 2-8: 160 vs 175 ± 5 ms [V-FT].
- SCM+ vs SCM- premotor RT -16.9 ms [95% CI -23.7, -10.1] (meta-analysis of 10 studies), comparable to FP-predictability effects [V-FT, Leow 2018].

Force:
- Force lower under high temporal preparation (short constant or long variable FPs), FPs 500/1,750/3,000 ms [V-ABS, Mattes & Ulrich 1997; V-FT of the description in Bausenhart & Ulrich 2026].
